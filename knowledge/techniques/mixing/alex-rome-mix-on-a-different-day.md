## What it is
A rule about when, not how: writing and mixing happen in separate sessions on separate days, because ears and
attention worn down by hours of writing cannot judge dynamics or frequency balance.

## When to use it
Every track. The moment the arrangement feels finished and the urge is to "just quickly mix it" is the moment to
stop.

## How
1. Finish writing, then stop for the day (said).
2. Sleep (said).
3. Open a session whose only purpose is the mix (said: "a dedicated mixing session").
4. Before touching anything in that session, listen once through and note what stands out (ours, inferred): fresh
   ears are spent within minutes, so the first pass is the valuable one.

## In Rohil's setup
End the writing day by leaving the next session what it needs: a full bounce (`rick-rubin logic bounce
bounces/<date>_premix.wav`), the numbers (`rick-rubin reference compare <dir> bounces/<date>_premix.wav`), and for a
song project `rick-rubin remember <dir> --outstanding "..."`. The mix session then starts from a measurement and a
list, not from memory. Rohil saves his own project; the agent never does.

## How to check it
Compare the premix bounce with the bounce at the end of the mix session: `rick-rubin reference compare` on both. The
second should have fewer lines outside the references' range. His verdict on the two files (`rick-rubin verdict`) is
the real test.

## Why it works
Hearing adapts to whatever it has been fed for hours: a harsh hat stops sounding harsh. A night resets that, and a
separate session stops arrangement tinkering from leaking into mix decisions (his reasoning,
[9:40](https://youtu.be/cU0tHoyJYjo?t=580), in our words).
