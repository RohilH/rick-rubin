## What it is
Three major chords and then the minor tonic: in relative-major terms IV–I–V–vi, the pop "feel-good" loop started on
its IV. He calls it the perfect euphoric progression.

## When to use it
A hands-up breakdown or a melodic drop that should feel warm rather than dark.

## How
1. Natural minor; degrees 6, 3, 7, 1 (said; in B minor: G, D, A, Bm; the first overlay at
   [5:05](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=305) reads G major).
2. Uneven chord lengths: 3 beats, 5 beats, 3 beats, 5 beats over four bars, so the second and fourth chords arrive a
   beat before the bar line (shown [5:26](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=326) on Ableton's ruler).
3. Third of each chord up an octave (said; shown [5:26](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=326)).
4. Two pedals above: scale degree 3, and the note a fifth above it, degree 7 (said; shown
   [5:36](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=336) as two held lines a fifth apart: D and A in B minor).
5. One more note added by ear (said; not readable in the frame).
6. Resulting colours (inferred): VI add9, III unchanged (the pedals are its root and fifth), VII with a suspended
   fourth, i as m7.
7. Add drums: he says the chords come alive only once the beat is under them (said,
   [5:46](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=346)).

## In Rohil's setup
The early arrivals must be written, not quantised away: the second and fourth chords start on beat 4 of bars 1 and
3. Pedals on their own track with a plucky patch (filter-envelope pluck card) against a pad on the chords.

## How to check it
`rick-rubin score <dir> --tracks <chords>`: changes at bar 1 beat 4, bar 3 beat 1 and bar 3 beat 4. Compare a bounce
of this rhythm with the same chords one per bar and record Rohil's verdict.

## Why it works
Three major chords in a row read as the relative major; landing on the minor tonic last keeps it from being sugary.
The pedals are the relative major's root and fifth, and the anticipations add push (inferred).
