## What it is
The same frame as the dark pump-up loop, but after the two-bar minor tonic it goes up to III and then to VI, both
major. He strips the thirds out and doubles the fifths, which keeps it hard while the root movement makes it happy.

## When to use it
High-energy drops that should feel bright and big rather than menacing; festival-style bass house sections.

## How
1. Natural minor; i for bars 1 and 2, III for bar 3, VI for bar 4 (said; shown
   [9:08](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=548) in G minor: Gm, Bb, Eb).
2. Take the third out of each chord, leaving root and fifth (said, [9:20](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=560)).
3. On a second synth sound, double the fifth of each chord above (said; shown
   [9:26](https://youtu.be/_yw85rAb6cw?t=566): D for two bars, then F; the third note was being moved in the frame
   and is inferred as Bb).
4. Same dark synth as the previous loop, big drums, a vocal (said).
5. To vary it (ours): bring the thirds back for the last pass before a breakdown to reveal the mode; alternate this
   ending with the dark one (VI then iv) every eight bars so the section has two faces.

## In Rohil's setup
Root-and-fifth chords are forgiving of distortion and heavy unison, where full triads turn to mud. A detuned saw
with a low-pass (pad card, more unison) or a reese layer under the roots both suit. Keep everything under 150 Hz
mono and check it.

## How to check it
`rick-rubin score <dir> --tracks <chords>`: no chord should contain its third. `rick-rubin width <dir>` on a bounce:
side energy in the low band should stay near zero even with heavy unison on the chords.

## Why it works
III and VI are I and IV of the relative major, so the second half of the loop is a major-key move. Without thirds
the tonic bars do not insist on minor, and stacked fifths sound open and strong (inferred).
